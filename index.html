Tangential Jet Flush Model

A fast model of how a rimless toilet flushes. Rim jets wash the bowl, swirl the pool and carry waste through the trap. Each flush computes in milliseconds, so you can change the design and see the effect on cleaning, waste removal, water use and relative noise right away. An AI lab searches for better designs and shows how the model would be trained and calibrated with high-fidelity simulation such as PreonLab, Ansys Fluent, Simcenter STAR-CCM+ or OpenFOAM. The coefficients are not calibrated yet, so read the results as indications for comparing designs, not as validated values.

Under the hood: a reduced-order model from cistern to trap, covering jet films on an oval bowl wall, pool swirl, washdown or siphonic outflow and waste transport. One flush takes about 20 to 40 ms in the browser; a free-surface CFD case of a flush runs for minutes to hours. How the model works · Why we built this · Slides: PowerPoint, PDF

Engineering challenge

How many expensive simulations does it take to reach the targets?

In development, each design candidate is checked with a high-fidelity simulation (CFD) or on a rig, and each run takes hours. Here a hidden reference model plays that part, and every run of it costs a virtual 8 hours. Three routes start from the same bowl and must reach the same targets. The fewer reference runs a route needs, the sooner the design is done.

Synthetic reference. It stands in for CFD: the flush model with other coefficients and effects the fast model does not have. It is not measured data.

  • Classic: one change at a time. An automated search: set the volume to the water target, then change one setting per run in a fixed, sensible order (jet position first); keep what helps, undo what does not, take smaller steps when nothing helps. A team using design of experiments would need fewer runs.
  • Fast model alone. The flush model screens a few hundred designs in seconds; the reference then runs its best designs in turn. It does not learn from the reference.
  • Fast model + learned correction (AI). After every reference run, a Gaussian process learns where and by how much the fast model is off. The next run goes to the design most likely to meet all targets under the corrected model.

      Fits the noise coefficients to recordings of real flushes. Record a few flushes of each of several designs, add them here with the design set up in the simulator, and the tool fits the coefficients so that the model's peak differences between the designs match the recorded ones. Only differences count: the microphone, the room and the distance cancel out. Audio is analysed in your browser and never uploaded.

      How to record
      1. Use one trap type. Comparisons across trap types are not robust.
      2. Put the phone or sound level meter at a fixed point, for example 1 m above the bowl, in a quiet room. Keep it there for all recordings.
      3. Start recording, flush, and stop once the bowl has refilled. Record three flushes of each design.
      4. Set the design up in the simulator, then add its recordings below. A sound level meter reading (A-weighted, fast, maximum) can be typed in instead of a file.
      5. If you also film the pool from above, note when an air core reaches the trap, in seconds after the flush starts, or type none.
      Coefficients to fit

      The demo makes synthetic recordings from a hidden reference: different coefficients, a valve click, fill valve noise, a low-pitched gurgle the model does not know about, and flush-to-flush scatter.

      A physics-informed neural network (PINN) learns from measurements and from the equations at the same time. Here it reads the pool level of one flush, as you would from a phone video of a ruler in the bowl, and finds two numbers the model needs: how fast the trap drains (the weir coefficient) and how long the water stays on the wall. The network describes the level over time; its training penalises both the misfit to the readings and any breach of the model's pool balance, and the two coefficients are learned with it.

      The measured flush is simulated: the full flush model with a weir coefficient of 1.45 instead of the model's 1.7, read every 0.1 s with 1.5 mm of reading noise. The network does not know these numbers.

      Train the network to see what it finds. It takes one to three seconds.

      A field surrogate predicts a whole result field, here the cleaning map of the bowl wall, from the design settings, in microseconds. It learns from expensive runs: proper orthogonal decomposition finds the typical map patterns, and a Gaussian process learns how much of each pattern a design has. This is the idea behind tools such as SimAI, Simcenter PhysicsAI or PhysicsNeMo. How many expensive runs does it need, and how does it compare with the fast physics model?

      Expensive runs are simulated: the synthetic reference of the engineering challenge, 8 virtual hours each, for 80 training designs and 20 unseen test designs of the oval bowl, varying the six challenge settings. Its maps come from the flush model with the reference's coefficients, so the fast model starts with an advantage here.

      How the model works

      1 · Cistern and jets

      The cistern drains through the flush valve and supply path into the rim nozzles. Jet speed follows the head above the nozzles, v = √(2gH / (1+K)), so flow falls as the cistern empties.

      2 · Wall film and cleaning

      Each jet is traced as a spreading band on the oval bowl wall. Gravity and turbulent wall friction act on it, and the wall holds it on its curved path. Where the band's lower edge meets the waterline, that part of it enters the pool with its swirl; the rest goes on round the wall. Wall shear τ = ½ρcfu² is accumulated per cell over the flush.

      3 · Pool swirl

      Film arriving at the pool brings angular momentum. The pool is treated as a rotating body that loses momentum through wall friction and outflow. Its swirl speed sets the vortex dip and how well floating paper is drawn to the outlet.

      4 · Outflow and waste

      A washdown bowl spills over the trap weir. A siphonic bowl first fills its trapway, then primes and draws the pool down until air breaks the siphon. Waste leaves when trapway velocity exceeds a transport threshold, limited by a maximum solids concentration.

      5 · AI lab

      Six tools use the model as their engine. An evolutionary optimiser searches the settings you allow for the best trade-off or for targets you set. A small neural network learns the model and answers a few thousand times faster. A hybrid calibration fits the coefficients to reference runs and adds a data-driven correction. A noise calibration fits the noise coefficients to recordings of real flushes. A physics-informed network finds a model coefficient from a pool level record, and a field surrogate predicts whole cleaning maps from expensive runs.

      Known limits

      • The bowl is an idealised oval with the same axis ratio at every depth. Real bowls change shape with depth and have the trap inlet off-centre.
      • The film is quasi-steady, stays attached to the wall and does not splash or interact with the other jet.
      • The pool rotates as a solid body. A real vortex core is deeper than the dip shown.
      • Waste is lumped into two classes. Clogging, media shape and drain-line carry are not modelled.
      • Splashing, jets hitting each other and water spilling over the rim are not modelled, so the model cannot say whether a design splashes or spills. It only flags when the pool level reaches the rim.
      • The reference data in the AI lab and the engineering challenge are synthetic (the model with other coefficients and extra effects) and labelled as such; the neural surrogate is a conventional network, and the physics-informed network solves only a small inverse problem. The CFD check is one simulated design, not a measurement. Nothing here replaces CFD or tests.
      • The presets are generic EU washdown and siphonic bowls and are not modelled on any manufacturer's product.
      • Status chips use display bands chosen for this tool, not values from a standard.
