Tangential Jet Flush Model

A fast model of how a rimless toilet flushes. Rim jets wash the bowl, swirl the pool and carry waste through the trap. Each flush computes in milliseconds, so you can change the design and see the effect on cleaning, waste removal, water use and relative noise right away. An AI lab searches for better designs and shows how the model would be trained and calibrated with high-fidelity simulation such as PreonLab, Ansys Fluent, Simcenter STAR-CCM+ or OpenFOAM. The coefficients are not calibrated yet, so read the results as trends.

Under the hood: a reduced-order model from cistern to trap, covering jet films on an oval bowl wall, pool swirl, washdown or siphonic outflow and waste transport. One flush takes 5 to 20 ms in the browser; a free-surface CFD case of a flush typically runs for hours. How the model works · Why we built this · Slides: PowerPoint, PDF

A small neural network (6 inputs, two hidden layers of 32, 3 outputs) learns the physics model from a few hundred example flushes, then answers in microseconds. This stands in for the real use case: training the same kind of network on high-fidelity runs, for example from PreonLab, Fluent, STAR-CCM+ or OpenFOAM.

Inputs are the same six design variables as the optimiser. Outputs are cleaned, solids removed and paper removed.

The calibration workflow from the model description, end to end. A hidden reference model plays the role of a high-fidelity solver: it uses different coefficients and adds effects the physics model cannot represent. The tool fits six coefficients to a few reference runs with Nelder-Mead, adds a Gaussian-process correction for what is left, and checks all three versions on designs it never saw.

The reference data is synthetic. With real high-fidelity runs, from any solver, the steps are the same.

    Fits the noise coefficients to recordings of real flushes. Record a few flushes of each of several designs, add them here with the design set up in the simulator, and the tool fits the coefficients so that the model's peak differences between the designs match the recorded ones. Only differences count: the microphone, the room and the distance cancel out. Audio is analysed in your browser and never uploaded.

    How to record
    1. Use one trap type. Comparisons across trap types are not robust.
    2. Put the phone or sound level meter at a fixed point, for example 1 m above the bowl, in a quiet room. Keep it there for all recordings.
    3. Start recording, flush, and stop once the bowl has refilled. Record three flushes of each design.
    4. Set the design up in the simulator, then add its recordings below. A sound level meter reading (A-weighted, fast, maximum) can be typed in instead of a file.
    5. If you also film the pool from above, note when an air core reaches the trap, in seconds after the flush starts, or type none.
    Coefficients to fit

    The demo makes synthetic recordings from a hidden reference: different coefficients, a valve click, fill valve noise, a low-pitched gurgle the model does not know about, and flush-to-flush scatter.

    How the model works

    1 · Cistern and jets

    The cistern drains through the flush valve and supply path into the rim nozzles. Jet speed follows the head above the nozzles, v = √(2gH / (1+K)), so flow falls as the cistern empties.

    2 · Wall film and cleaning

    Each jet is traced as a spreading band on the oval bowl wall. Gravity and turbulent wall friction act on it, and the wall holds it on its curved path. Where the band's lower edge meets the waterline, that part of it enters the pool with its swirl; the rest goes on round the wall. Wall shear τ = ½ρcfu² is accumulated per cell over the flush.

    3 · Pool swirl

    Film arriving at the pool brings angular momentum. The pool is treated as a rotating body that loses momentum through wall friction and outflow. Its swirl speed sets the vortex dip and how well floating paper is drawn to the outlet.

    4 · Outflow and waste

    A washdown bowl spills over the trap weir. A siphonic bowl first fills its trapway, then primes and draws the pool down until air breaks the siphon. Waste leaves when trapway velocity exceeds a transport threshold, limited by a maximum solids concentration.

    5 · AI lab

    Four tools use the model as their engine. An evolutionary optimiser searches the settings you allow for the best trade-off or for targets you set. A small neural network learns the model and answers about a thousand times faster. A hybrid calibration fits the coefficients to reference runs and adds a data-driven correction. A noise calibration fits the noise coefficients to recordings of real flushes.

    Known limits

    • The bowl is an idealised oval with the same axis ratio at every depth. Real bowls change shape with depth and have the trap inlet off-centre.
    • The film is quasi-steady, stays attached to the wall and does not splash or interact with the other jet.
    • The pool rotates as a solid body. A real vortex core is deeper than the dip shown.
    • Waste is lumped into two classes. Clogging, media shape and drain-line carry are not modelled.
    • Splashing, jets hitting each other and water spilling over the rim are not modelled, so the model cannot say whether a design splashes or spills. It only flags when the pool level reaches the rim.
    • The AI lab uses a conventional neural network surrogate, not a physics-informed network (PINN), and its calibration demo runs on synthetic data. Nothing here replaces CFD or tests.
    • The presets are generic EU washdown and siphonic bowls and are not modelled on any manufacturer's product.
    • Status chips use display bands chosen for this tool, not values from a standard.
