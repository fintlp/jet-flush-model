0 · How to read this page

There are two kinds of entries. Cited sources back a specific statement or number in the simulator or the model description, for example a runtime or a test requirement. Background sources are standard textbooks and papers for the physics used in a sub-model. They explain the kind of equation used, not the exact coefficient values, which are uncalibrated estimates.

Model coefficients are not taken from any of these sources. They are reasonable starting values, to be replaced by values fitted to CFD or test data as described in the calibration section.

1 · Flush tests and standards

  1. BackgroundBS EN 997:2018. WC pans and WC suites with integral trap. British Standards Institution, 2018. European standard for construction, performance and test methods of WC pans with integral trap and close-coupled suites. Used for: the European context of washdown bowls, flush volumes and test methods. Standard summary
  2. CitedU.S. EPA WaterSense. WaterSense Performance Overview: Tank-Type Toilets. 2022. States that WaterSense toilets must clear 350 g of soybean paste test media and four balls of toilet paper in four of five flushes, that the test is part of ASME A112.19.2/CSA B45.1, and that voluntary MaP testing goes up to 1,000 g. Used for: the solids and paper test load idea and the "transport capacity" result. PDF
  3. BackgroundASME A112.19.2/CSA B45.1. Ceramic Plumbing Fixtures. North American standard that contains the waste extraction test referred to by WaterSense. Used for: the North American siphonic context.
  4. CitedUS 8,438,670 B2. High performance siphonic toilet capable of operation at multiple flush volumes. US patent. Reports measured water surfaces of two wash-down dual-flush toilets (about 115 to 131 cm²) and two siphonic dual-flush toilets (349 and 528 cm²), 1.5 L of water at rest in one wash-down toilet, seal depths of 5.4 to 6.4 cm after refill, and trapway diameters of about 47 to 54 mm. Used for: the plausibility of the presets' water surface, pool volume and seal (method section 4), and the trap diameter range in checks/literature.js. Google Patents
  5. CitedSBZ and Hochschule Esslingen. Practical flush test of rimless WCs. Independent test of rimless and swirl-flush WCs that combines parts of the EN 997 flush tests with practical tests; the flush water is calibrated to 2.1 L/s and a 20 g sawdust test shows where the water reaches. Used for: the upper end of the mean flush flow in checks/literature.js, and the sawdust test as a simple validation of film reach. Test description
  6. CitedUS 9,353,512. High performance toilet with rim-jet control capable of enhanced operation at reduced flush volumes. US patent. A comparative CFD example of a 4.8 L jet-driven siphonic toilet with a 60 mm trapway reaches a siphon peak of 4.4 L/s at 0.58 s. Used for: the siphon peak outflow comparison in checks/literature.js. USPTO PDF

2 · Hydraulics

  1. BackgroundChow, V. T. (1959). Open-Channel Hydraulics. McGraw-Hill. Used for: the broad-crested weir law Q = C L z1.5 for flow over the trap weir.
  2. BackgroundHenderson, F. M. (1966). Open Channel Flow. Macmillan. Used for: weir and critical-flow relations.
  3. BackgroundIdelchik, I. E. (1996). Handbook of Hydraulic Resistance, 3rd edition. Begell House. Used for: lumped loss coefficients K for valve, flush pipe, nozzles and siphon.
  4. BackgroundWhite, F. M. Fluid Mechanics. McGraw-Hill, any recent edition. Used for: the energy balance behind the jet speed v = √(2gH / (1+K)) and orifice flow through the trapway.

5 · Toilet flushing CFD

  1. CitedCADFEM. Simulation leads Geberit to design a toilet bowl with asymmetrical geometry. CADFEM Journal. Describes CFD-led development of an asymmetrical bowl with a lateral inlet, more than 100 virtual design drafts, and about one day per design iteration. Used for: the industrial turnaround time in the comparison of approaches and the example of one working day per iteration in the savings calculator, and the peak flow range in checks/literature.js (free-outlet cisterns up to 2.4 L/s, about 2.0 L/s in the TurboFlush bowl, below 1.0 L/s in some bowls). It also links the quieter flush to low-turbulence flow with less air mixed in, the idea behind the noise indicator. Article
  2. CitedLi et al. (2020). Siphon pipe parameter optimization of the toilet using CFD-DEM coupling method. Uses Ansys Fluent with the VOF model coupled to EDEM for polypropylene test balls, with a mesh of about 2.3 million cells and a 0.2 ms time step. Used for: the example of mesh-based CFD with discrete solids for toilets. Paper page
  3. BackgroundLopes, A. G., & Costa, V. A. F. (2019). Using CFD in the design process of a toilet bowl. Journal of Fluid Flow, Heat and Mass Transfer, 6, 1–8. Transient free-surface CFD in Ansys CFX, validated against flow visualisation. Used for: typical CFD setup and validation of bowl flushing, and the lower end of the mean flush flow in checks/literature.js (flushes simulated at 0.85 and 0.58 L/s). doi:10.11159/jffhmt.2019.001
  4. CitedManav, S., Kemaneci, H. İ., & Şimşir, B. (2023). Comparison of two toilet bowls with the use of CFD. Journal of Fluid Flow, Heat and Mass Transfer, 10. Ansys CFX with VOF and the k-ε model, about 0.8 to 0.9 million nodes, a 0.1 ms time step over 10 s; compared qualitatively with a sawdust test after EN 997. Used for: typical mesh, time step and validation level of VOF toilet studies, and most comparisons in checks/literature.js: peak inflow 1.3 and 1.5 L/s, inflow over after 6.5 and 5.4 s, about three turns of the water on the bowl wall, up to 1.6 and 3.1 L in the bowl, 1.0 to 1.3 L at rest, outflow from 0.5 and 1.75 s, and peak wall shear of 1.75 and 2.95 Pa. The paper also states that the wall was treated as frictionless, so its shear figures are indicative only. PDF
  5. CitedSalguero, E., Cabrera-Moreta, V. H., & Gaibor, I. (2025). Hydraulic performance validation of a 4.8 L low-consumption toilet using CFD simulation. Preprints.org, doi:10.20944/preprints202507.1579.v1 (not peer reviewed). Runs the same bowl in OpenFOAM and Ansys Fluent with VOF; both give siphon formation at about 5 s, but peak velocities of 3.0 and 4.8 m/s and siphon pressures of −3.7 and −5.3 kPa, with no experimental comparison. Used for: OpenFOAM as an option, and how far two solvers can differ on one bowl. Preprint
  6. CitedStudy on the influence of toilet siphon pipe shape on flushing performance. Journal of Engineering and Technological Sciences (ITB). VOF simulations of siphon toilets with experimental validation; flushing performance peaks for siphon pipe diameters of 50 to 53 mm rather than improving with size. Used for: the trap diameter comparison in checks/literature.js, where the model shows no such optimum. Article

9 · Noise and air entrainment

  1. BackgroundMinnaert, M. (1933). On musical air-bubbles and the sounds of running water. Philosophical Magazine, 16(104), 235–248. Used for: the natural frequency of a bubble and the idea that the sound of running water comes from bubbles.
  2. BackgroundLeighton, T. G. (1994). The Acoustic Bubble. Academic Press. Used for: bubble acoustics in general, and why bubble formation is the main sound source of low-speed water flows.
  3. CitedKiger, K. T., & Duncan, J. H. (2012). Air-entrainment mechanisms in plunging jets and breaking waves. Annual Review of Fluid Mechanics, 44, 563–596. Reviews onset speeds (about 0.7 to 1 m/s for planar jets) and entrainment correlations, including Brattberg and Chanson's. Used for: the onset speed Ue, the exponent 1.8 and the size of ke. PDF
  4. CitedBrattberg, T., & Chanson, H. (1998). Air entrapment and air bubble dispersion at two-dimensional plunging water jets. Chemical Engineering Science, 53(24), 4113–4127. Gives Qa/Qw = 7.7·10⁻⁴ (h/d − 1.04) ((U − Ue)/√(g d))1.8 for speeds up to 4 m/s, as quoted by Kiger and Duncan. Used for: the entrainment law of noise sources 1 and 2.
  5. CitedErvine, D. A. (1985). Two phase flow in hydraulic structures: a review of bubbly and air pocket flows in civil engineering hydraulic structures. Lecture series, U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, report PAP-488. Reports onset speeds of about 0.8 to 1.0 m/s for rough turbulent jets and about 3.6 m/s for smooth ones. Used for: the default onset speed of 0.8 m/s for the turbulent films and outflow in a bowl. PDF
  6. BackgroundGordon, J. L. (1970). Vortices at intakes. Water Power, 22, 137–138. Empirical critical submergence of intakes, Sc = C V √d. Used for: the cross-check of the air core criterion.
  7. CitedGökmener, S. (2016). Investigation of critical submergence at single and multiple horizontal intake structures having air-entraining vortices. MSc thesis, Middle East Technical University. Reviews Gordon's rule in the form Sc/d = 1.70 Fr (symmetric) and 2.27 Fr (asymmetric approach), and Reddy and Pickford's rule. Used for: the coefficient of Gordon's rule in SI units. PDF
  8. BackgroundIEC 61672-1:2013. Electroacoustics: Sound level meters, Part 1: Specifications. International Electrotechnical Commission. Used for: the 125 ms "fast" time weighting of the noise trace and peak.
  9. CitedVilleroy & Boch. Sound measurement certificate, TwistFlush WC. Test bench measurement at Fraunhofer IBP to DIN 4109-1 and VDI 4100: the plumbing sound level in a receiving room of a two-storey test installation, as a fast-weighted maximum LAF,max, normalized as those standards require and valid for the installation conditions stated in the certificate. It reports 23 dB(A) against a DIN 4109 minimum requirement of 30 dB(A). Used for: how WC installation noise is rated in building acoustics, and why the indicator reports a fast-smoothed peak. It does not validate the noise indicator, which covers only the water in the bowl and trap. PDF
  10. CitedQuerido, J. G., & Bistafa, S. R. Sound pressure level evaluation from toilet flushes. Proceedings of Inter-Noise 96. Finds that the opening of the flush valve gives the noise peak of a pressure-fed toilet flush. Used for: the limit that valve noise is not covered. PDF
  11. BackgroundGeberit. Under control. Case of drainage noise in a residential building traced to a 90° bend and carried through the structure; the level fell from about 38 to 25 dB after decoupling. Used for: why pipe and building noise are out of scope. Article
  12. BackgroundRose, M. T. (2019). Noise control of vacuum-assisted toilets. Master's thesis, Brigham Young University. Measures flush noise in phases, with peaks at valve opening and closing. Used for: background on how flush noise is measured. Thesis page

11 · Development chain and ceramic manufacturing

  1. CitedOide, K., Mihara, Y., Kobayashi, T., Saitou, T., Umeda, M., Matsubara, S., & Terada, K. Simulation of the firing process for ceramic products. Mechanical Design & Analysis Corporation, LIXIL Corporation and Tohoku University, 2018, published by Dassault Systèmes. Finite element simulation of a sanitary ware piece during firing: about 3 % shrinkage in drying and about 10 % in firing, creep under the piece's own weight, predicted displacements mostly within 1 to 2 % of the measured ones and up to 3 % at some points. Notes that the casting form is commonly designed by experience. Used for: the shrinkage figures and the statement that the fired shape can be predicted to within a few percent, in the development chain on the why page. PDF
  2. CitedHopa, D. Y., & Özel, E. (2012). Evaluation of pyroplastic deformation in sanitaryware porcelain bodies. Ceramics International, 38(2). Studies how the body's composition changes pyroplastic deformation, the sagging of a sanitary ware piece under its own weight at firing temperature, measured with a fleximeter; more mullite raises the viscosity and reduces the sagging. Used for: the statement that a piece sags under its own weight in the kiln, in the development chain on the why page. DOI
  3. CitedDigitalfire Reference Library. Firing shrinkage. Glossary entry by Tony Hansen: vitreous porcelain bodies shrink more than 10 % in firing, measured from dry to fired, against 7 to 8 % for whitewares and 3 to 4 % for earthenwares; total shrinkage is a little less than the sum of drying and firing shrinkage. Used for: a second, independent source for the firing shrinkage of about 10 % in the development chain on the why page. Entry
  4. CitedCeramic Ninja. Sanitary ware manufacturing production process. Step-by-step description of sanitary ware production: body and glaze preparation, moulds, casting, drying, inspection, glazing, firing in a tunnel kiln at about 1,250 °C, sorting, refiring and packing. Used for: the series production step in the development chain on the why page. Article
  5. CitedEN 997:2018, table of contents (sample). Lists the requirements and tests for WC pans: water seal, bowl washing, paper and ball flushing, splash, after-flush volume, water absorption, static load, durability, and conformity assessment with type testing and factory production control. Used for: the tests and certification steps in the development chain on the why page. Sample
