Why we built this · Tangential Jet Flush Model

Why are we doing this?

Engineering rarely slows down because people run out of ideas. It slows down because every idea waits for a simulation, a prototype or a test slot. This simulator is a small, concrete example of a different way to work: answer most questions in seconds, and spend the slow, expensive tools only where they make the decision.

Peter Fintl · 19 October 2026

The bottleneck is the loop, not the solver

In a typical product development, a design question goes into a queue. Someone builds a CFD case or a prototype, it runs overnight or for days, and the answer comes back when the team has already moved on. Geberit, for example, reported about one day per design iteration for its bowl development, with more than a hundred virtual drafts before the first prototype.

That is fast by the standards of physical testing. But it means a team can only afford to ask a few questions, so most design decisions are still made on experience before any number exists. And the later a weak decision is found, the more it costs to change: in the tool, the mould, the production line.

Three speeds of simulation, used together

The answer is not to replace CFD. It is to put the right tool at each stage and connect them, so that slow tools are only used for the few designs that deserve them.

1 · Fast physics

A reduced-order model keeps the main mechanisms in a few equations: here the cistern, the jet films, the pool swirl, the trap and the waste. It is explainable, it runs anywhere, and engineers can argue with it. That makes it a thinking tool, not only a calculator.

2 · AI on top

Optimisation searches the design space for trade-offs and targets. Neural surrogates learn expensive models and answer in microseconds. Hybrid models combine physics with a data-driven correction. None of this needs the physics to be perfect; it needs it to point the right way.

3 · High fidelity where it counts

PreonLab, CFD and the test rig check the few designs that survive. Their results flow back to calibrate the fast model and train the surrogate. Each expensive run is used twice: for the decision, and to make every later answer better.

From idea to engineering to manufacturing

Idea

Test a concept in the meeting where it comes up. Make the bowl oval, move the jets, cut a litre of water, and see the effect before the discussion ends. The guided demo shows this in one minute.

Engineering

Narrow the design space before booking CFD or rig time. Set the targets that matter, such as water per flush, cleaning and waste removal, and let the optimiser find candidates. Spend the slow tools on five designs, not fifty.

Manufacturing

Check how robust a design is to what production will really deliver: nozzle tolerances, glaze, small shape deviations. When one variant takes milliseconds, sweeping thousands of tolerance combinations is cheap. Rig and field data from production then recalibrate the model.

The tolerance study is the natural next feature for this simulator. The design sweep already shows the principle on two parameters at a time.

Beyond classical numerical methods

Classical simulation answers one precise question per run. The methods combined here answer many approximate questions per second, and say how far to trust them:

  • Reduced-order physics instead of resolving every eddy, so the mechanism stays visible.
  • Evolutionary optimisation instead of one-at-a-time parameter studies.
  • Neural surrogates that answer in microseconds once trained on solver results.
  • Hybrid calibration that keeps the physics and lets data correct what it misses, with an uncertainty that grows away from known designs.

What this does not do

It does not replace validation. The model on this page is not calibrated yet, so its numbers show trends between designs, not certified values. AI suggestions are candidates for a CFD or rig check, never the final answer.

Some things are out of scope: splashing, jets hitting each other and spill-over are not modelled, so the page cannot say whether a design splashes. The AI is a conventional neural surrogate, not a physics-informed network, and the calibration demo uses synthetic data. The presets are generic bowls, not any manufacturer's product.

The gain is in how quickly a team gets to the right few questions.

The toilet is the example, not the limit

A flush is a good showcase because it combines free-surface flow, thin films, swirl and transport of solids, and everyone understands the result. The same pattern applies wherever fluids or other physics make design loops slow:

Vehicle water managementrain, wading and washer fluid on body, sensors and seals
Lubrication and coolingoil splash in gearboxes, e-motor and battery cooling
Appliancesdishwashers, washing machines, coffee machines
Process and cleaningtank cleaning, spray systems, clean-in-place
Building servicesdrainage, HVAC, water-saving fittings
Sensors and ADASkeeping cameras and lidar clean and dry

How a team would start

  1. Pick one recurring design question that today waits for CFD or tests.
  2. Write down the main mechanisms with the people who know them, and build a fast model around them.
  3. Calibrate it with CFD or test data you already have, and check it on cases it has not seen.
  4. Put it in front of engineers as a simple web tool, so it gets used in design meetings, not only by specialists.
  5. Add AI and close the loop: optimisation and surrogates on top, and every new high-fidelity run feeding back into the model.
