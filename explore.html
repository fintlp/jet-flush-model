Explore the simulator yourself

A guide for simulation engineers who want to try the model on their own questions, and for colleagues who show it to clients. Each recipe says what to set, where to look and how to read the result, with a link into the page.

Peter Fintl · 22 October 2026

Start in five minutes

  1. Open the simulator and pick a preset: EU washdown or siphonic.
  2. Press Play flush and read the four result cards: cleaning, waste removal, water use and relative noise.
  3. Press Pin as reference. From now on every change is compared with this design, until you clear the pin.
  4. Move one slider, for example Ovality in Bowl and trap. The green and red chips show how much each result moved. Undo steps back.
  5. Open the advanced groups at the bottom of the settings: Model coefficients and Noise model. These are the values a calibration would fit, not design choices.

Everything runs in your browser. Nothing you set or enter is sent anywhere; your last design is remembered in this browser only.

Recipes for simulation engineers

Each recipe answers one question. They need no installation, except the last two, which use Node.js.

1What does one change do?

Where: Simulate, Results, Traces

  1. Pin the current design as reference.
  2. Change one setting and watch the chips on the result cards.
  3. Open Flush traces to see why: flow in and out, bowl level, pool swirl, waste left, noise. Click a trace to jump the playback to that moment.

Read it as: the direction and size of the change, against your pinned design. The values themselves are not calibrated.

2Map a trade-off between two settings

Where: Design sweep

  1. Choose two settings for the axes and one result, for example nozzle area and jet angle against cleaning.
  2. Run the sweep: 144 flushes on a 12 × 12 grid in one to two seconds. The best cell is outlined.
  3. Click a cell to load that design into the simulator and look at it in full.

Watch out: the sweep retraces the films less often than the simulator to save time, so a loaded design can differ from its cell, usually by less than half a point of cleaning. The sweep offers design settings only; for the model coefficients see recipe 3.

3How much do the uncalibrated coefficients matter?

Where: Model coefficients · advanced, in the settings; or examples/coef-sensitivity.js

  1. Pin your design, then change one coefficient, for example the critical soil shear τc or the shear dose to clean.
  2. For all 13 coefficients at once, run node examples/coef-sensitivity.js washdown 0.25 ovality=1.35: each coefficient is moved 25 % down and up, one at a time, and the changes are listed, largest first.

What it shows: which coefficients to calibrate first for that design. For the round washdown preset, cleaning moves by about 2 points with τc or the shear dose; for the same bowl made oval (ovality 1.35), by 6 to 8 points. Paper removal depends most on the paper transport threshold and removal rate. Method section 12 lists what each coefficient controls and what to fit it against.

4Is the design robust to production tolerances?

Where: Tolerance study

  1. Check or edit the tolerances: nozzles and jets, bowl shape, valve height, supply path loss, flush volume. The defaults are assumptions.
  2. Run 40, 100 or 200 varied copies of the current design.
  3. Read the spread of each result and "Spread mostly from": the tolerances that explain most of it.

Watch out: a low R² in the chart means thresholds or interactions matter, and the shares are rough. A spread smaller than about one point of cleaning is within the model's own resolution.

5Find designs that meet your targets

Where: AI lab, Design optimiser · full tutorial: Design optimiser

  1. Choose which settings may change: jets, flush, bowl geometry.
  2. Choose Meet targets and set them, for example at most 4.0 L, at least 88 % cleaned, a noise limit of "no louder than now".
  3. Optional: Robust mode rates each design at the low end of its tolerance spread (about seven times slower).
  4. Run, then load a recommended design into the simulator.

Watch out: about 540 quick estimates in about 10 seconds, then the recommended designs are checked at full resolution. The sliders allow valve heights and nozzle areas beyond real cisterns; check the peak flow of a result (recipe 10, or the flow trace) before you trust it.

6How does the fast model compare with full CFD?

Where: Checked against CFD, or part 2 of the story (#cfd-tour)

  1. Play the wetting maps: CFD left, fast model right, the wall unrolled.
  2. Switch to the cleaning view and read "Where they differ".

Read it as: one OpenFOAM simulation of the washdown preset on two meshes, a second simulation, not a measurement. Totals are close (cleaned 89 % against 86 %); the pattern of uncleaned areas is not (11 % overlap). Use the fast model for totals and trends, not for cell-by-cell maps.

7See how calibration would work

Where: AI lab: Hybrid calibration, Noise calibration, Physics-informed network, Field surrogate · full tutorials: Optimiser and AI lab tutorials

  • Hybrid calibration: six coefficients fitted to a few reference runs with Nelder-Mead, plus a Gaussian process for what is left, checked on unseen designs.
  • Noise calibration: fits the noise coefficients to recordings; the only tool that accepts your own data (audio files or meter readings, analysed in the browser).
  • Physics-informed network: finds the weir coefficient from a pool level record and the model's equation.
  • Field surrogate: predicts whole cleaning maps from 10 to 80 expensive runs.

Read it as: the workflow, not real accuracy. The reference data are synthetic (the model with other coefficients and extra effects), and each tab says so. Method sections 13 and 14 describe how a calibration against real CFD or rig data would run.

8How many expensive runs does a design task take?

Where: Engineering challenge, then What it saves

  1. Play the race: three ways of working, eight starts of the same task, each expensive run costing a virtual 8 hours.
  2. In Your turn, use the simulator as your fast model and send designs to the reference yourself. Can you beat the AI route?
  3. Put your own figures into the calculator in What it saves.

Read it as: the mechanism (a fast model that learns from every expensive run needs fewer of them), against a synthetic reference with five settings to change.

9Compare a round bowl with a European wall-hung pan

Where: the preset list in Simulate, Results, the section drawing

  1. Pin the EU washdown preset as reference.
  2. Choose the preset EU wall-hung: an oval opening of 330 × 275 mm, a small pool of 125 × 100 mm set 25 mm towards the front, 175 mm from the rim to the water, and a 70 mm trapway whose crest is the bottom of the round bore.
  3. With the same jets it cleans about 9 points less, and the pool rises higher before the trap carries the flow. Set the pool offset to 0, or the trap crest to flat, to see what each feature costs: the offset about 5 points, the round crest about 2.
  4. The jets were laid out for the round bowl. Run the design optimiser on the wall-hung pan to see what jets suit it.

Read it as: a representative pan, not a product. Its sizes come from a catalogue drawing, two data sheets and EN 997; the trapway loss of 1.5 velocity heads is an estimate until the CFD trapway runs give a fitted value.

10Run many designs outside the browser

Where: examples/batch-csv.js in the repository; needs Node.js 18 or later

git clone https://github.com/fintlp/jet-flush-model.git cd jet-flush-model node examples/batch-csv.js 1000 out.csv 1000 washdown designs, about a minute node examples/batch-csv.js 300 sip.csv siphonic the siphonic preset node examples/batch-csv.js 300 wh.csv wallhung the EU wall-hung preset

The script varies the settings listed at its top (edit them), spreads the designs evenly over each range (Latin hypercube, fixed seed) and writes one row per design: the settings, cleaning, rim line, wetted area, solids, paper, water, seal, peak flow, the noise peak and whether the run was valid. The model itself is one call: FlushModel.simulate(settings, options), described in the README and method section 17.

11Check the model yourself

Where: checks/ in the repository

node checks/physics.js runs 61 physics checks: energy and angular momentum of a frictionless film, the water balance, closed-form cases, re-integration with Runge-Kutta, grid and time-step convergence. node checks/literature.js compares the presets with published toilet studies. checks/README.md lists all checks with what they test.

Read it as: the model solves its own equations correctly. It does not show that the equations describe a real bowl; that needs calibration.

The settings at a glance

44 settings in seven groups. Every slider has an i button with a short explanation; method section 12 gives defaults, units and what to fit each coefficient against.

GroupSettingsWhat they are
Cistern and flush4Full and partial flush volume, valve height above the jets, supply path loss. Together they set the jet speed and the flush duration.
Tangential jets5Number of jets, nozzle area and height, downward angle, position round the rim.
Bowl and trap9Ovality, rim and pool radius, rim-to-waterline depth, seal depth, trapway diameter; from 2.2 the pool offset towards the front, the trap crest (flat weir or round bore) and the trapway loss before the crest.
Siphon5Siphon drop and loss, trapway capacity before priming, water needed to prime, bowl refill. Used only for siphonic bowls.
Test load2Solid media and paper in the pool at the start.
Model coefficients · advanced13Film friction and spreading, soil shear and shear dose, swirl transfer and losses, transport thresholds, removal rates, weir coefficient. Uncalibrated defaults.
Noise model · advanced6Coefficients of the relative noise indicator. Uncalibrated defaults.

Where to be careful

The model is physics-based but not calibrated. These are the places where it is known to be blind or off; the model description (sections 4, 13 and 16) has the details.

If you askWhat the model doesRead it as
Is the wall-hung preset a real product?Sizes from a catalogue drawing, two data sheets and EN 997; the trapway loss is an estimate.A representative European wall-hung pan. Compare it with the round bowl, not with a product test.
Does this design splash or spill?Splashing, jets hitting each other and spill-over are not modelled; only a pool reaching the rim is detected.No answer on splashing. Check fast jets in CFD or on the rig.
Very fast jets (high valve, low path loss, large nozzles)The sliders go beyond real cisterns: about a third of random designs exceed the 2.4 L/s a free-outlet cistern delivers.Check the peak flow before trusting such a design.
Which trapway diameter is best for a siphonic bowl?Priming does not depend on the trapway diameter, so removal simply falls with diameter. A published study finds an optimum at 50 to 53 mm.No trap-size optimum from this model.
Full against partial flush in the washdown presetAll cleaning happens in the first 1.1 s or so, so both buttons clean the same area.The buttons differ in waste removal and refill only.
When does the water reach the pool?Later and more at once than in CFD (0.54 s against 0.14 s); the swirling pool does not wash the wall near the waterline.Early wetting and the band at the waterline are weak spots.
Where exactly does the wall stay dirty?Totals are close to CFD; the uncleaned areas overlap only 11 % with the CFD's.Use totals and trends, not cell maps.
How much water is in the bowl at rest?The basin below the waterline has an assumed shape; real bowls hold about twice as much.Pool volume and swirl inertia are rough.
An asymmetric bowl or an off-centre trapThe oval has the same axis ratio at every depth, and the trap sits in the centre.Not represented.
Is design A quieter than design B?Noise is a relative indicator from four sources, with a range over uncertain coefficients; comparisons across trap types are not robust.Trust a direction only when the card says Quieter or Louder.
What is the real cleaning value?All coefficients are uncalibrated defaults.Compare designs; do not quote absolute values.

For colleagues: showing it to clients

The stance

The simulator does not show that we know bowls better than the client's engineers, who have developed them for generations. It shows how a fast physics model and AI can speed up their way of working, and that we want to develop that method together with them. Their knowledge goes into the model; our methods make it usable faster.

Questions you will hear

Is it calibrated?
No. The coefficients are physics-based defaults. Calibrating them against the client's CFD or rig data would be the first step of a real project; recipe 3 shows which coefficients matter most.
Does it replace CFD?
No. It decides where CFD is worth running, and every CFD run improves it. Part 2 shows one CFD comparison, honestly, including where they differ.
Are the 6 fewer iterations real?
They come from a synthetic design task, labelled as such, and show the mechanism. Every step of the calculation is on the why page; the calculator takes the client's own figures.
Why a toilet?
It combines free-surface flow, thin films, swirl and solids, and everyone understands the result. The same pattern fits vehicle water management, lubrication, appliances or sensor cleaning; the why page has a table.
Is our data safe?
Everything runs in the browser. Nothing entered on the page, including figures in the calculator or recordings in the noise calibration, is sent anywhere.
What would it take for our product?
One recurring design question, a fast model of its main mechanisms, calibration with data they already have, then the loop. That is what a workshop looks at.
