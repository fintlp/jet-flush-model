Model description · Tangential Jet Flush Model

How the jet flush model works

A reduced-order model of a rimless WC flushed by tangential rim jets. It traces each jet as a film on the bowl wall, follows the pool level, swirl and trap flow, and estimates cleaning, waste removal, water use and relative noise. One flush takes about 5 to 20 milliseconds to compute in a web browser.

1 · Summary

The model answers three questions for a given bowl and flush design, in this order of priority:

  1. Cleaning. How much of the bowl wall above the waterline receives enough wall shear from the jet films to count as cleaned.
  2. Waste removal. How much of a solid test load and of floating paper leaves through the trap.
  3. Water use. How much water the flush takes, and whether the water seal is restored afterwards.

A fourth result, noise, is an experimental, relative indicator: is the design louder or quieter than a reference, which part of the flush makes the noise, and how sure the model can be with its assumed coefficients. It is described in section 10.

It does this by splitting the flush into a chain of simple physical sub-models that each have a clear meaning: cistern, jets, wall film, pool, trap and waste. Each sub-model uses a few equations instead of solving the full three-dimensional flow. That is why one flush computes in milliseconds rather than the hours a free-surface CFD case needs.

What the numbers mean today

All coefficients are physically reasonable estimates, not yet fitted to measurements or CFD. The model shows how designs compare and which way a change pushes the result. Absolute percentages and grams become trustworthy only after calibration against a few reference runs, as described in section 13.

Typical uses are screening many design variants quickly, explaining the flush mechanism to non-specialists, and choosing which few variants deserve a full CFD or rig test.

2 · Using the simulator

The simulator is built to be tried, not read. Every change recomputes the flush in milliseconds and shows how much it helped or hurt.

Getting started

  • Start here: the strip under the intro gives the three steps: set up a bowl, play the flush, let the AI lab search.
  • Demo me: the large button in that strip starts the guided walkthrough. Once you scroll past the strip, a small Demo me button appears in the section menu instead, so there is always exactly one on screen.
  • Section menu: stays at the top while you scroll and jumps to Simulate, Results, Traces, Wall map, Sweep, Tolerances, CFD check, Challenge, AI lab or How it works.

Changing the design

  • Presets: EU washdown (4.5/3 L) and siphonic (4.8 L flush plus 0.8 L bowl refill, so 5.6 L per full flush in the water use; the refill restores the full seal after both flush buttons). Full or partial flush is switched next to the preset.
  • Sliders: grouped into cistern and flush, tangential jets, bowl and trap, siphon, test load, model coefficients and noise model. Each has an i button with a one-line explanation. The coefficient and noise groups are marked advanced; they hold the values that calibration would fit, not design choices.
  • Rim and pool radius: the pool radius always stays at least 25 mm smaller than the rim radius, so the bowl wall keeps a width. The slider being moved stops at that gap; the other radius is never changed. In the design sweep, cells that break it are hatched and not simulated.
  • On phones: the sliders open in a drawer from the Adjust design button. The bar at the bottom always shows cleaning, solids and water with their change, so the effect is visible while you edit.

Seeing the effect

  • Estimates and validity: every result card is labelled as an estimate. If a design overflows the bowl or its flush does not finish within 60 s, a red notice above the results says so and the chips read Overflow or Incomplete. In the design sweep such cells carry a red cross.
  • AI results after changes: if you change a setting that an AI tool did not vary itself, for example the bowl after training the surrogate, its panel says the results are for the earlier configuration and asks you to run it again.
  • Change chips: each result card shows a green or red chip with the change, for example +5 or −12, and briefly pulses when its value moves. For water, less is shown as an improvement.
  • What the chips compare with: by default, the design before your last edit. A series of quick slider moves counts as one edit, so the chip shows the effect of the whole adjustment.
  • Pin as reference: fixes the current results as a reference. Every change after that is compared with it, until you clear the reference.
  • Noise card: marked experimental. The big number is the peak noise in dB against a reference, not a sound level. Without a pinned design the reference is the preset of the same trap type and flush button, named in a grey tag under the number; with a pinned design it is that design, marked with a pin in a blue tag. Below it is the sampled range over the uncertain noise coefficients. The status chip reads Quieter or Louder only when the whole sampled range agrees, Similar when it stays close to zero, and Unclear otherwise. The rows say which source makes the peak and which makes most of the sound energy, and when an air core or a siphon gurgle occurs. When no design is pinned, the small chip next to the number shows the change against the design before your last edit, like on the other cards.
  • Playback: Play flush replays the flush at 1×, ½× or ¼× speed. After a change, the views show the end of the flush, so the wall map shows the final cleaning result.

Changing a setting or a tolerance after a study greys it out until it is run again.
  • Checked against CFD: the fast model next to an OpenFOAM simulation of the same bowl and jets, on the same unrolled wall: when each part of the wall gets wet (with a time slider and playback) and what gets cleaned, the computing time of both, and where they differ. Described in section 13.
  • Engineering challenge: how many expensive high-fidelity runs three routes need to bring an oval bowl to the targets, played out on a virtual clock, with a "Your turn" mode. Described in section 14.
  • AI lab: design optimiser, neural surrogate, hybrid calibration, noise calibration, physics-informed network and field surrogate, described in section 14.

3 · Model at a glance

Every 10 ms of simulated time, the model updates the cistern, the jets, the pool level, the trap flow, the swirl and the waste load. It re-traces the jet films on the wall whenever the jet speed or the pool level has changed noticeably, and at least every 0.25 s. Water leaving the nozzles reaches the pool only after its travel time on the wall; in between traces, the swirl and splash it brings follow the actual flow at every step, and the shear dose of each interval is the mean of the footprints at its start and end.

4 · Bowl geometry

Equivalent radius profile

The bowl wall is described by an equivalent radius R(z) at each depth z below the rim, running from the rim radius Rrim to the pool radius Rpool at the static waterline, a depth D below the rim:

R(z) = R_pool + (R_rim − R_pool) · cos(π z / 2D), 0 ≤ z ≤ D

The wall is vertical at the rim, as on a rimless lip, and slopes inward towards the pool. At the default values the wall meets the waterline at roughly 43° from vertical.

Oval plan shape

At every depth the plan section is an ellipse with axis ratio e (the ovality slider, long axis divided by short axis). Its semi-axes are chosen so the ellipse has the same area as a circle of radius R(z):

a(z) = R(z) · √e b(z) = R(z) / √e π a b = π R(z)²

Keeping the area constant means pool volumes, levels and flow rates do not change when only the ovality changes. That isolates the effect of the shape on the film and the swirl. The wall surface can then be written as one implicit equation, which the film tracer uses directly:

F(x, y, z) = √(x²/e + e·y²) − R(z) = 0 F < 0 inside the bowl

The outward wall normal is the normalised gradient of F. Positions around the rim are given as the ellipse parameter angle θ, where 0° is the end of the long axis and 90° the middle of the long side.

Pool and seal

The pool level zp is measured from the trap weir crest, which equals the static waterline. Above the crest the pool area follows the wall profile. Below it the basin narrows linearly to half the pool radius at the seal depth ds. The model pre-computes the volume above or below the crest as a function of level, so each time step converts a volume into a level with one table lookup. A second table holds the pool's polar moment for the swirl (section 8).

For the washdown preset the water surface at rest is 121 cm² and the pool below the crest holds 0.44 L. Published measurements of two wash-down dual-flush toilets give water surfaces of about 115 to 131 cm², so the preset fits there; one of them held 1.5 L of water at rest, apparently including the trapway, which suggests the model's basin is too small by a factor of about two. Two siphonic dual-flush toilets in the same source had water surfaces of 349 and 528 cm², against 201 cm² for the siphonic preset. The basin shape below the waterline is therefore a calibration item: it affects the swirl inertia, the siphon draw-down and the refill needed to restore the seal.

5 · Cistern and jets

The cistern has a constant cross-section Ac (0.035 m²). Its outlet sits a height Hcr above the rim nozzles. The head at the nozzles is the water depth in the cistern plus that height, and the jet speed follows from an energy balance with one lumped loss coefficient K for valve, flush pipe and nozzle:

H = V_c / A_c + H_cr v_j = s(t) · √( 2 g H / (1 + K) ) Q_in = n · A_n · v_j n jets, nozzle area A_n each dV_c/dt = −Q_in

The valve opens linearly over 0.15 s, expressed by the factor s(t). It closes once the flush volume has left. The cistern always starts full: a partial flush releases its smaller volume from a full cistern, as a dual-flush valve does, so its head and jet speed start at the full-flush values. With the default washdown preset this gives a peak inflow of about 1.7 L/s and a flush of about 3 s for 4.5 L.

Each jet leaves its nozzle tangent to the rim in the horizontal plane, tilted downward by the jet angle α. The jets are spaced evenly around the rim, starting at the jet position angle.

6 · Wall film and cleaning

What is traced

Each jet is followed as a band of water sliding along the wall: a centreline, and a width across the flow that grows as the film spreads. When the jets start, the film front advances along the traced path, and only the part of the wall the front has reached is wetted. The film is treated as quasi-steady: its path depends on the current jet speed and pool level. It is traced again whenever the jet speed has changed by more than 3 % or the pool level by more than 3 mm since the last trace, when the film starts or stops, and at least every 0.25 s. The design sweep and the optimiser search use 10 %, 10 mm and 0.6 s.

The nozzle sits 4 mm below the rim, and the band starts across the nozzle's width. For a wide nozzle part of that width would lie above the rim; that part is folded onto the wall instead, so the band starts narrower and none of the jet flow is lost above the rim. The film thickness is still limited to the nozzle height, as everywhere on the wall.

Forces on the film

Two forces act along the wall. Gravity pulls the film down, and turbulent wall friction slows it. Friction is expressed through a friction factor cf and the local film thickness h:

a = g·ẑ − a_f · u ẑ points down a_f = c_f · |u| / (2h) h = min( h_noz , Q_j / (|u| · w) ) continuity, w = film width w = w₀ + k_sp · ℓ spreads with distance ℓ travelled; w₀ is the part of the nozzle width on the wall

The wall itself keeps the film on its curved path. In the round bowl, centrifugal force presses the film against the wall, and on an inward-sloping wall it can carry a fast film back up towards the rim after a first dip, so a film may circle the bowl more than once before it reaches the pool. Moving to a smaller radius would raise the film's angular speed if its angular momentum were kept, but wall friction removes angular momentum all along the path: in the washdown preset the film enters the pool with about a third to two thirds of the angular momentum it left the nozzle with. In an oval bowl the curvature changes around the rim, so the film is pressed harder at the narrow ends than along the flat sides.

Integration on the curved wall

The tracer moves the band's centreline in three dimensions and keeps it on the wall. This handles round and oval bowls with the same code. Per step of 6 mm arc length:

  1. Compute the force from gravity and friction and keep its part along the wall; the wall reaction keeps the film on the wall.
  2. Advance the velocity over the step time Δt = 6 mm / |u|, and the position with the mean of the old and the new velocity.
  3. Pull the position back onto the wall with two Newton steps on F(x, y, z) = 0.
  4. Turn the velocity into the new tangent plane with the rotation that turns the old wall normal into the new one, so the wall changes the film's direction but not its speed.
  5. Set the speed from the energy balance over the step: the work of gravity minus the work of wall friction.
  6. Widen the film and stamp the part of its footprint that lies between the rim and the waterline onto the wall grid.

A film that climbs back to the rim meets the rim lip; it loses its upward speed there and slides along below the rim. The checks in checks/physics.js confirm the integration: without friction, a film in a round bowl keeps its energy to within 0.001 % and its angular momentum about the bowl axis to within 0.3 % over 3 m of path, and on a vertical wall it falls as in free fall to within 0.2 mm over 190 mm. In 1.3 the speed was updated with the slope at the start of each step and the velocity was cut back to the wall and rescaled, which lost up to about a fifth of the kinetic energy over 3 m without friction and steered the film downwards.

Where the film enters the pool

The band does not enter the pool all at once. At every step, the share of its width that lies below the current waterline enters the pool there, at the waterline radius, with its flow, angular momentum and velocity. The band left on the wall is re-centred on the part that remains: its centreline moves up across the band to the middle of that part, and its width shrinks to it. A film whose centreline just misses the pool on its first dip therefore already hands over its lower part there, and a small change in jet speed or pool level changes the hand-over by a small amount. In 1.3 the whole film entered where its centreline reached the waterline, so the same small change could switch the film between entering on the first dip and circling once more, which made the pool swirl jump.

The trace ends when the whole band has entered the pool, when the film has slowed below 0.05 m/s, or after 500 steps (3 m of path). Flow still on the wall in the last two cases counts as lost: it reaches the pool eventually, but brings no swirl and no splash. In 300 random designs, at least 95 % lost no flow at all.

Wall shear and shear dose

The wall above the static waterline is divided into 90 cells around the rim and 40 cells down the wall. Each cell has its true surface area on the oval wall. Each film step stamps the rectangle the band sweeps: one step length along the flow and the band width w across it, worked out in wall coordinates (arc length round the rim and down the wall). Every cell whose centre lies inside it, between the rim and the waterline, receives the wall shear stress of the film. Counting cells by their centres makes the covered area converge as the grid is refined: against a grid four times finer each way with a 1 mm film step, cleaning differs by about 0.2 points in the median and at most 0.9 points over 23 designs. In 1.3 a cell counted as soon as any sample point touched it, which overstated cleaning by about 2 points on average and up to about 5 points on this grid.

τ = ½ · ρ · c_f · |u|² J = ∫ max(0, τ − τ_c) dt shear dose, Pa·s cell cleaned ⇔ J ≥ J_crit

The critical shear τc represents the stress below which soil does not come off, and Jcrit how long it must be exceeded. Both describe the soil, not the bowl, and are the main targets of a wash test calibration.

Cleaning results

  • Wall area cleaned: area-weighted share of cells with J ≥ Jcrit.
  • Wall wetted: share of cells the film touched at all.
  • Line 25 mm below the rim: share of the row of cells at 25 mm depth that is cleaned. This mirrors the common ink-line wash test.
  • Cleaned per litre: cleaned share divided by water used, a simple efficiency figure for comparing variants.

7 · Pool level and outflow

The pool follows a plain volume balance. Its inflow is the film water arriving from the wall, plus the bowl refill after a siphonic flush:

dV_p/dt = Q_arr + Q_refill − Q_out dV_wall/dt = Q_in − Q_arr Q_arr = Q_in + (V_wall − Q_in · τ̄) / τ̄ τ̄: mean travel time of the films; none before the front arrives

The water on the wall tends to the steady amount for the current paths, nozzle flow times mean travel time, with that travel time as time constant. In the washdown preset about 1 L is on the wall during the flush, and the first water reaches the pool about 0.5 s after the valve opens. When the pool rises and the paths get shorter, the wall water drains faster; when the valve closes, it drains out. Outflow is never allowed to take more water than the basin holds.

Washdown bowl

Water spills over the weir crest into the trapway. The weir flow uses a broad-crested weir law, capped by what the trapway can pass as an orifice:

Q_weir = C_w · L_w · z_p^1.5 z_p > 0, C_w = 1.7 m½/s, L_w = 70 mm Q_cap = 0.6 · A_t · √( 2 g (z_p + d_s) ) Q_out = min(Q_weir, Q_cap)

The level cannot drop below the crest, so a washdown bowl always keeps its full seal.

Siphonic bowl

Before priming, water spills over the weir and part of it drains by gravity down the trapway, up to a capacity Qg. Any excess fills the trapway. When the stored volume reaches the priming volume Vprime, the siphon starts and the outflow switches to full-bore flow driven by the drop Hs below the weir:

dV_trap/dt = Q_weir − Q_g before priming, V_trap ≥ 0 Q_siphon = A_t · √( 2 g (z_p + H_s) / K_s ) siphon breaks when z_p reaches −d_s air reaches the trap inlet

The outflow of the step in which the pool reaches the trap inlet is limited, so the level ends exactly at the inlet, and the siphon breaks there. While a primed siphon draws the pool down, the time step is split into sub-steps whenever more than 2 % of the pool would leave in one step, so the level, the moment of the break and the spin-up of the shrinking pool do not depend on the time step. The siphon can prime again if enough water arrives later; the first priming and the last break are reported.

After the siphon breaks, the remaining film water and the bowl refill raise the level again. The refill runs in at 0.1 L/s once the valve has closed, as from the fill valve's refill tube. The seal after the flush is the simulated level at the end above the trap inlet, capped at the seal depth, so the pool level trace ends at the reported seal. Water left in the trap leg when the siphon breaks is not returned to the bowl. A seal below 50 mm is flagged, whether it comes from the flush or from a seal depth set below 50 mm.

Overflow

If inflow exceeds what the weir or siphon can take for long enough, the pool rises. The level table runs up to the rim; water beyond it is counted as spilled over the rim. Such a design is marked as overflowing, its results are shown as not valid, and the optimiser never counts it as a solution. Splashing over the rim before the pool is full is not modelled.

8 · Pool swirl

Film entering the pool brings angular momentum about the bowl axis, ℓ = x·vy − y·vx per unit mass, taken where each part of the band enters; ℓarr is the flow-weighted mean over the jets, with lost flow counted as zero, and it arrives with the film water Qarr (section 7). The pool is treated as a body rotating at angular speed ω with angular momentum L = I·ω:

dL/dt = η · ρ · Q_arr · ℓ_arr − (Q_out / V_p) · L − T_f I = ¼ · ρ · (e + 1/e) · ∫ π r(z)⁴ dz elliptic sections, from the bottom of the basin to the level T_f = ½ · ρ · c_fp · (1 + k_ov (e − 1)) · A_wet · (ω R_c)² · R_c A_wet = π R_c² + P_e · (d_s + 0.03 m + max(z_p, 0)) floor plus side wall of the pool P_e = π R_c · [ 3 (a + b) − √((3a + b)(a + 3b)) ] Ramanujan's perimeter, a = √e, b = 1/√e
  • η is the share of the incoming angular momentum that survives the impact and mixing at the waterline.
  • The outflow term removes angular momentum with the water that leaves.
  • Tf is turbulent friction on the wetted pool surface Awet: the water surface area plus a side wall as high as the seal depth, 30 mm below the trap inlet and the level above the crest. The perimeter of the elliptic section with the same area as a circle of radius Rc uses Ramanujan's approximation, which is accurate to better than 0.01 % for these ovalities. The ovality term kov represents the extra loss of a rotating flow in a non-circular basin, where the flow must keep changing direction.

The moment of inertia is integrated over the narrowing basin. In 1.3 it used the surface radius for the whole pool, which overstated it by a factor of 1.6 to 1.8, so the swirl came out too low and the dip too small.

The results shown are the swirl speed at the pool edge, vs = ω Rc, and the solid-body vortex dip, vs² / 2g, both as peaks over the time the trap carries flow. After a siphon break the sump left in the bowl can spin up while nothing drains; the solid-body pool does not describe that state well, and the swirl then affects neither waste removal nor the air core. The dip is a lower bound, because a real vortex has a concentrated core that dips deeper.

How much an oval bowl slows the swirl is set entirely by the assumed coefficient kov: with kov = 0 the peak swirl hardly changes between a round and a strongly oval bowl. The oval-bowl swirl trend is an input of the model, not a result, until kov is calibrated.

The swirl also enters waste removal through a swirl Froude number and a centring factor that saturates at strong swirl:

Fr_s = v_s² / (g R_c) C_s = Fr_s / (Fr_s + Fr₀)

9 · Waste transport

Waste is lumped into two classes that behave differently, similar in spirit to media tests with soybean-paste style solids and paper.

Solid media

Solids leave once the mean trapway velocity ut = Qout / At exceeds a transport threshold. This is the velocity in the trap's rising leg, which in both bowl types runs full of water up to the weir; behind the weir a washdown trapway runs part full. A wider trapway therefore needs more flow for the same transport speed, while the benefit of its larger passage for bulky waste is not modelled. The removal rate is the smaller of a kinetic rate, proportional to the remaining mass, and a capacity limit set by the maximum volume fraction of solids the flow can carry:

f_S = clamp( (u_t / u_cS − 1) / 0.5 , 0, 1 ) dM/dt = −f_S · min( c_max · ρ_s · Q_out , k_S · M · (1 + β C_s) ) capacity = ∫ f_S · c_max · ρ_s · Q_out dt grams the flush could carry

The capacity figure is comparable in idea to a maximum-load media test result: it states how much solid load this flush could clear, independent of the load actually placed.

Paper

Paper floats and has to be drawn down into the trap. Its removal depends on the trapway velocity and on how well the swirl centres it:

f_P = clamp( (u_t / u_cP − 1) / 0.5 , 0, 1 ) dN/dt = −f_P · k_P · N · ( φ₀ + (1 − φ₀) · C_s )

With φ₀ = 0.2, a flush without swirl removes paper at one fifth of the rate of a strongly swirling one at the same trap velocity.

10 · Noise indicator

The noise result is an experimental, relative indicator. It answers two questions: is this design louder or quieter than a reference, and which part of the flush makes the noise. It does not give a sound level in dB(A), and it has not been checked against recordings yet. It covers sound made by the water in the bowl and the trap. The cistern, the fill valve, the pipes and the building are out of scope; they often dominate what a neighbour hears, but they depend on the installation, not on the bowl.

Because several of its coefficients are assumed, every noise result comes with a sampled range that shows how far it moves when those coefficients change. A direction (quieter or louder) is only shown when the whole sampled range agrees.

Why the model counts air bubbles

At the speeds in a flush, about 1 to 3 m/s, most of the sound of running water comes from air bubbles. A bubble rings briefly when it is formed, close to its Minnaert frequency:

f₀ = (1 / 2πR) · √( 3 γ p₀ / ρ ) ≈ 3.3 kHz at R = 1 mm

So the noise rises with the rate at which air is drawn under the water. The model writes every source as a flow of air into the water, Qa in m³/s, and takes the radiated sound power as proportional to it. Large air pockets in the trap make less sound per litre of air than fine splash bubbles, so they carry a weight wtrap:

W(t) = Q_a1 + Q_a2 + w_trap · ( Q_a3 + Q_a4 )

This proportionality is the central assumption. It is reasonable for comparing similar flushes, where the bubbles are of similar size, and weakest when sources of very different character are compared, such as splash against a siphon gurgle.

The four sources

1 · Jet films entering the pool. Where a film meets the pool, the model knows the film velocity from the film trace and the velocity of the pool surface at that point, which rotates as a solid body with angular speed ω. What counts is the speed relative to the surface: a film that slides into a pool turning the same way pulls in much less air than one that hits still water. Plunging water starts to entrain air above an onset speed Ue of about 0.8 to 1 m/s for turbulent jets, and the air flow then grows with about the 1.8th power of the excess speed up to about 4 m/s (Brattberg and Chanson, reviewed by Kiger and Duncan). Applying this law to a wall film is an extrapolation beyond the conditions it was measured for: the correlation comes from free jets that fall several jet thicknesses and plunge steeply, while a wall film slides into the pool at a shallow angle with no free fall. The model uses it because no correlation for wall films entering a pool was found. In the presets this source is small (section 10, below), so the extrapolation matters most for designs where films hit a slow pool hard.

U_rel = | u_film − ω ẑ × r | Q_a1 = k_e · Σ_parts Q_k · (U_rel − U_e)^n U_rel > U_e, over the parts of each film entering the pool

2 · Water falling into the outlet. Water that passes the trap weir drops by hfall before it lands in the outlet. Its landing speed combines critical flow over the crest with the free fall, and the same entrainment law applies. This source runs only while the trap flows with a free surface: always in a washdown bowl, and before priming and after the break in a siphonic bowl. While a siphon runs full, it is silent in the model.

U_fall = √( 2 g z_p / 3 + 2 g h_fall ) Q_a2 = k_e · Q_out · (U_fall − U_e)^n

3 · Air core at the trap. Swirl lowers the pool surface over the trap. The pool swirl model gives the angular momentum of the pool. Near the trap the swirl is assumed to concentrate into a Rankine vortex: a solid-body core of radius rc = a · Rc inside, a free vortex outside. The vortex is built so that it carries the same angular momentum as the pool. For a cylinder of uniform depth this fixes its edge speed at ve = vs / (2 − a²), where vs is the edge speed of the solid-body pool. Integrating the radial pressure balance then gives the depth of the vortex centre below the mean water level:

Δh = G(a) · v_s² / 2g G(a) = F(a) / (2 − a²)² F(a) = (2/a² − 1)(1 − a²) + ½ + 2 ln a + 1 − a² G(1) = 0.5 (solid body), G(0.6) = 1.13, G(0.45) = 2.1

An air core reaches the trap when the vortex centre dips down to the top of the trap inlet while water is flowing out, that is when Δh ≥ zp + ds. While it is open, air is drawn into the trap at a share ccore of the outflow, Qa3 = ccore · Qout. The model assumes that the vortex centre sits over the trap inlet; in a real bowl the inlet is off-centre and the vortex is drawn towards it. The criterion is a heuristic built from a simple vortex, with an assumed core size, not a validated prediction.

4 · Siphon break. When the siphon breaks, air rushes into the trap leg. The model lets air replace the water in the leg at the rate the siphon was carrying it away, decaying once the leg's volume has been replaced:

Q_a4 = Q_brk · exp( −(t − t_b) · Q_brk / V_prime )

Here Qbrk is the siphon flow just before the break, tb the time of the break and Vprime the priming volume, the water the trapway holds when the siphon starts. Since the break always happens when the pool reaches the trap inlet, its strength depends mainly on the trap: seal depth, trapway, siphon drop and priming volume.

From sound power to the result

  • Smoothing: W is smoothed exponentially with a 125 ms time constant, WF ← WF + (W − WF) · (1 − e−Δt/τ). This mirrors the "fast" setting of sound level meters (IEC 61672-1), but it is applied to the air flow proxy, not to a measured sound pressure.
  • Peak level: 10 · log₁₀ of the largest smoothed W during the flush. This is the big number on the card.
  • Sound energy: 10 · log₁₀ of the time integral of W. It also counts how long the flush is loud.
  • Reference: both are shown as differences in dB from a reference. Without a pinned design, the reference is the preset of the same trap type and the same flush button, computed with the current model coefficients so that only the design differs. With a pinned design, it is that design, and the card marks this with a pin. Cards, noise trace and its band always use the same reference.
  • Where it comes from: the card names the source with the largest share of the smoothed sound power at the moment of the peak, and separately the source with the largest share of the sound energy over the whole flush. They can differ, for example when a short gurgle makes the peak but the outlet makes most of the energy.

Only differences between designs are used, so a factor that scales every source equally cancels. The coefficient ke does not cancel: it scales only the two splash sources, so it sets their balance against the trap sources together with wtrap and ccore. That balance is the main uncertainty and the first thing to calibrate.

How to calibrate it

  1. Record three to five flushes of each of at least three designs of the same trap type, with a sound level meter or a calibrated phone app at a fixed point, for example 1 m above the bowl in a quiet room. Note the A-weighted fast maximum of each flush.
  2. Compare the differences between designs, not the absolute values, with the model's peak differences.
  3. Film the pool from above at the same time to see when an air core reaches the trap, and fit the core size a.
  4. Fit hfall to the trapway drawing, then wtrap and ccore to the recordings, starting with a siphonic bowl if there is one, because its gurgle separates the trap sources from the splash sources.
  5. Once fitted, narrow the uncertainty spans in noiseCorners() in model.js to what the fit supports.

The Noise calibration tab of the AI lab carries out steps 2 to 4 (section 14). It takes audio files or sound level meter readings per design, together with the air core times from video, and fits the chosen coefficients.

Limits

  • Not a sound level: no spectrum, no A-weighting, no room, no microphone position. Only differences between designs are meaningful, and only those of the same trap type are reasonably robust.
  • No noise from the cistern, the flush or fill valve, the pipes or the building structure. Valve opening can be the loudest moment of a pressure-fed flush (Querido and Bistafa).
  • No splashing droplets, no jets hitting each other, no film breaking away from the wall.
  • The air core does not change the trap flow in the model: it adds noise but does not slow the siphon or change waste removal.
  • The vortex centre is assumed to sit over the trap inlet, and the vortex shape is reconstructed from the pool's angular momentum for a cylinder of uniform depth.
  • The uncertainty range covers five coefficients, not the form of the model.

13 · Calibration with high-fidelity simulation

The coefficients of this model are calibrated against a few reference runs of a high-fidelity free-surface solver, ideally backed by a few rig measurements. The workflow below does not depend on the tool: any solver that can export the quantities listed in step 2 will do. The choice of tool matters for how well it captures the thin wall films and the wall shear, how much a run costs, and whether it can carry test media.

Two families of solvers

  • Mesh-based, with an interface-capturing method such as volume of fluid (VOF) or level set: the bowl is filled with a volume mesh, and a field marks where the water is. Mature, widely validated and the method of every published toilet study found for this page. A thin wall film needs several cells across its few millimetres, so the mesh near the wall must be fine, and splashing droplets smaller than the cells are lost.
  • Particle-based, smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH): the water is a cloud of particles and no volume mesh is needed. Free surfaces, splashing and fast-changing water shapes come naturally, and the methods run well on GPUs. Wall shear in a thin film needs particles much smaller than the film thickness and careful treatment of the wall.

For either family, check the wall shear of a thin film on a simple case before trusting the shear map of a flush, for example a film running down an inclined plate, against a known solution or a measurement.

Calibration procedure

  1. Choose a small set of reference designs that span the design space you care about, for example low and high nozzle area, two jet angles and two ovalities.
  2. Run them in the reference solver and export: inflow Q(t) at the nozzles, wall wetting and wall shear maps at fixed times, pool level and outflow over time, pool angular momentum, and the media removed over time.
  3. Fit hydraulics first: K and the valve height from Q(t), then the weir and siphon coefficients from level and outflow.
  4. Fit the film next: cf and ksp from the wetted footprint, then τc and Jcrit from the wall shear map combined with a soil threshold or a physical wash test.
  5. Fit swirl: η, cfp and kov from pool angular momentum.
  6. Fit waste last: thresholds, rates and cmax from removal curves, ideally at two or three loads.
  7. Hold out one or two runs that were not used for fitting and compare. If the error is acceptable, the model can screen designs between the reference points.

Each fit is a small least-squares problem over a handful of coefficients. Because one model run takes milliseconds, a standard optimiser such as Nelder-Mead can run thousands of evaluations in seconds.

On the page: the CFD check

The first high-fidelity reference is an OpenFOAM interFoam (volume of fluid) simulation of the washdown preset, built from the model's own bowl profile and driven by the model's jet inflow; the case, its runs and their limits are kept with the CFD files in the cfd/ folder. In that run the trap outflow follows the fast model up to 1.3 s; after that the outlet draws air from the swirling pool and the bowl holds more water, which the page says. Both are put on the model's wall grid (90 sectors round the rim, 40 rows from the rim to the waterline) and compared area-weighted:

  • When the water gets there: the first time each cell is wet. The CFD samples every 0.05 s and calls a cell wet when more than half of its wall faces hold more than half water; the model takes the first time a film footprint covers the cell centre. A time slider and a playback show the wetted area growing on both sides.
  • What gets cleaned: the shear dose J = ∫ max(0, τ − τc) dt with the model's τc = 3 Pa, cleaned where J reaches 1.5 Pa s, over the run's window. In the CFD every wet wall face counts, so the swirling pool washing the wall near the waterline counts too; in the model only the films clean.
  • Computing time: the CFD's wall-clock time on the processor cores used, against the fast model's time for the same window measured in the browser.
  • Where they differ: first water in the pool, film paths, the band near the waterline, and pool swirl (whose two definitions differ, so only the trend is compared). Curves of wetted share and swirl over time are shown for engineers.

Every view carries the caveat that this is a second simulation, not a measurement of a real bowl: the mesh is named, thin films and fine spray are smoothed at that resolution, and the trap outflow is prescribed from the model, so the CFD checks the wall and the pool, not the trap. Agreement shows consistency, not truth. Stills and a video of the CFD flush (ParaView renders of the water surface coloured by speed, listed in cfd/washdown/media/manifest.json) are shown in the panel.

Hybrid option

Where the physics-based model stays off after calibration, a small machine-learning correction can be trained on the remaining differences between model and the reference runs, for example a Gaussian process per output. This needs far fewer reference runs than a full neural surrogate, keeps the physical explanation, and gives an uncertainty estimate that grows away from the reference designs.

14 · AI lab

The simulator has an AI lab with six tools, and the engineering challenge on the page puts several of them to work together. All use the physics model described above as their engine, and all run in the browser. They show different roles AI and data can play next to a physics model: searching designs, replacing the model with a faster learned copy, correcting the model with data, fitting it to measurements, learning from measurements and equations at once, and predicting whole result fields from expensive runs.

Engineering challenge

The AI lab tools show single techniques. The challenge shows what they are for: fewer expensive runs before a design is done. A hidden reference model stands in for high-fidelity runs (CFD or a rig), and every run of it costs a virtual 8 hours, one run at a time. The fast flush model costs milliseconds and is not counted.

  • Task: start from the EU washdown bowl made oval (1.35) with the jets at the ends of the long axis, 4.5 L, which reaches 74.5 % cleaning in the reference. Reach at most 4.0 L, at least 87 % cleaning, 95 % solids and 80 % paper, with the seal restored. Nozzle area, jet angle, jet position, nozzle height, valve height and flush volume may change. After the start design, every route uses the full water budget of 4.0 L, so the search is over the other five.
  • Reference (synthetic): the flush model with other coefficients (film friction 0.010 instead of 0.008, critical shear 3.3 instead of 3.0 Pa, cleaning dose 1.6 instead of 1.5 Pa s, solids transport threshold 0.38 instead of 0.35, solids concentration limit 0.11 instead of 0.12) and effects the fast model does not have: cleaning lost at high valve heights (splash and jet break-up, up to about 5 points), away from a best nozzle size and for steep jets, and less solids removal for small nozzles at low volume. On average it cleans about 3 points less than the fast model, with a spread of about 2 points. The page labels it synthetic and lists the differences on request. The OpenFOAM reference (section 13) is meant to anchor it later.
  • Classic route, one change at a time: sets the volume to 4.0 L, then moves one setting per run by a quarter of its range, jet position first, then valve height, nozzle area, jet angle and nozzle height. A move that brings the design closer to the targets is kept and repeated; one that does not is undone and the other direction tried; after a round without progress all steps halve. Closeness is the sum of the shortfalls in points.
  • Fast model alone: simulates 300 random designs at 4.0 L, refines three times around its four best with 10 new designs each, and runs its best designs in the reference in order of predicted shortfall. It does not learn from the reference.
  • AI route, fast model plus learned correction: after every reference run it learns the difference between reference and fast model for cleaning, solids and paper: a linear trend by ridge regression and a Gaussian process on what is left (squared-exponential kernel, length scale by leave-one-out error from five runs on, noise 1 point, about the fast model's own numerical scatter). The corrected prediction with its uncertainty gives each candidate the probability of meeting all three targets; the next run goes to the most likely one, among the 300 screened designs and 40 new ones around the four best. While no design is likely to meet the targets, this favours designs whose correction is still uncertain, so the route explores instead of circling one spot. This is multi-fidelity Bayesian optimisation in its simplest form.
  • Eight races: one race depends on the start design and on chance in the screening, so the page shows eight. Race #1 starts from the design above with the informed classic order; races #2 to #8 from seven other start designs, with other random choices in the screening and refinement and a random order for the classic route. Runs to target: AI route median 4.5 (2 to 13), classic route median 12 (2 to 26); the fast model alone was sometimes the quickest (2 runs) but missed within 60 runs in 3 of 8. In total, 43 runs for the AI route against 110 for the classic route. In race #1 itself the fast model alone happens to be quickest (3 runs, AI 5, classic 11). An untouched second set of eight seeds, not shown on the page, gave AI median 4, classic 11.5, and 4 misses of the fast model alone, so the tuning of the routes did not fit these eight.
  • Charts on one clock: Play runs a virtual clock over ten days. The first chart counts how many of the eight projects each route has finished; the second shows, for the race picked in the table, the best cleaning reached so far, every run as a dot (filled when it meets the other targets); the third shows, for the same race, what the AI route expected before each run (mean and two standard deviations) against what the reference gave.
  • Stored and recomputed: the eight races are stored in challenge-data.js, so the race plays at once. Any race can be recomputed live with the same code in a web worker, without blocking the page (about 20 to 40 s); the page then says whether the result is identical. After the first playback, race #1 is recomputed this way automatically. checks/challenge.js recomputes all eight and fails if they differ from the stored ones.
  • Guided tour: nine steps from the problem to what it means, started by the Guided tour button, from the last step of Demo me, or with #challenge-tour at the end of the address. It plays the clock, shows a race where the fast model alone gets lost, explains the AI route's predictions, starts the live recomputation, and makes a first reference run in "Your turn". Like Demo me, it gives the visitor's own design back at the end.
  • Your turn: the visitor uses the simulator as the fast model, loads the start design, and sends designs to the reference one at a time. Only the six challenge settings are taken from the simulator.

What this does and does not show: with a fast model that is right on trends but off in places, learning the difference from each expensive run reaches the targets reliably with few runs, while the fast model alone is a gamble and the classic route is slow. The numbers depend on this synthetic reference and on five settings; they are an illustration, not a benchmark. The algorithms are written out in challenge-core.js.

Physics-informed network

A physics-informed neural network (PINN) solves an inverse problem: from a pool level record of one flush, as read from a phone video of a ruler in the bowl, it finds the weir coefficient and the time water spends on the wall.

  • Physics: the model's pool balance A(z) dz/dt = Qarr(t) − Cw Lw max(z, 0)1.5, with the inflow Qin(t) known from the cistern, the pool area A(z) a quadratic fit to the bowl's volume table, and the arrival Qarr the inflow delayed by the wall: Qin convolved with s/θ² e−s/θ, θ = τ/2, which is the same as two equal stores in series with a mean delay τ. Unknown: Cw and τ.
  • Network: time to level, two hidden layers of 20 tanh units. The loss is the misfit to the readings (weight 10), the squared residual of the balance at 120 points over 8 s, and the start value z(0) = 0. Cw and τ are trained with the weights (Adam, 1,000 epochs, about 1.5 s in the browser). The time derivative of the network is carried through the forward pass, and a hand-written backward pass gives the gradients through both; no library is used (pinn-core.js).
  • Data: simulated. The full flush model with Cw = 1.45 instead of 1.7, read every 0.1 s (81 readings) or 13 times over 8 s, with 1.5 mm of noise, or a record that stops after 3 s.
  • Comparisons: the same network fitted to the readings alone, and classic least squares that integrates the same balance for trial values of Cw and τ (Nelder-Mead).
  • Result: from the whole flush both find Cw within a few percent of 1.45 (network about 1.42 to 1.44, least squares 1.49 to 1.50, over five noise draws 1.39 to 1.44 and 1.45 to 1.51), where the default 1.7 is 17 % off; the network also estimates the water on the wall, about 0.8 L at 1 s against 0.95 L in the full model. Both are a few percent off because the simple balance does not capture every detail of the full model's wall flow. When the record stops after 3 s, Cw cannot be identified (the filling alone cannot separate a slow drain from a long wall time), which the page says; the physics still keeps the predicted level after the cut much closer to the truth than the network without physics (checks/pinn.js).

For a problem this small, least squares is faster (tens of milliseconds) and the better tool, and the page says so. PINNs pay off when the equations are expensive to solve repeatedly or partly unknown, or the data are scattered in space and time. For a full three-dimensional flush with turbulence and free surfaces, high-fidelity CFD with surrogate models is the realistic route.

Field surrogate

A field surrogate predicts a whole result field from the design settings; here the cleaning map, the shear dose over the threshold J/Jcrit capped at 3 on the 90 × 40 wall grid. The training and test data are expensive runs of the engineering challenge's synthetic reference: 80 training and 20 unseen test designs of the oval bowl, Latin hypercubes over the six challenge settings (field-core.js).

  • Data surrogate: proper orthogonal decomposition of the training maps (method of snapshots, Jacobi eigen-decomposition of the snapshot matrix, 10 modes) and a Gaussian process per mode coefficient, length scale by leave-one-out error. This is the idea behind field surrogates trained on CFD runs alone, such as SimAI, Simcenter PhysicsAI or PhysicsNeMo.
  • Fast model + correction: the same, trained on the difference between the reference and the fast model's maps, added to the fast model's map.
  • Scores on the unseen designs, area-weighted: the share of the cleaned area matched (intersection over union) and the error of the cleaned share.
  • Result: the data surrogate matches 81 % with 10 runs and 85 % with 80 runs (27 virtual days), missing the cleaned share by 9 to 13 points. The fast model alone matches 95 % and misses by 1.6 points, without any expensive run; with the learned correction 95 to 96 % and 1.4 to 1.5 points. A whole map from the data surrogate takes about 0.1 ms, from the fast model about 30 ms (checks/field.js).

The honest reading: a field surrogate has to learn the physics from examples and needs many expensive runs; a fast physics model starts far ahead. The correction adds little here because the synthetic reference is the same model with other coefficients, so its maps differ mostly in level, not in pattern; the CFD check shows real pattern differences that a correction trained on CFD runs could learn.

Design optimiser

An evolutionary multi-objective search in the style of NSGA-II. The user decides what it may change and what counts as good.

  • What may change: any mix of nozzle area, downward jet angle, jet position, nozzle height, number of jets, valve height and flush volume, plus the bowl geometry: ovality, rim radius, pool radius, rim-to-waterline depth, seal depth and trapway diameter. Each setting is searched over its full slider range. Everything not ticked stays at the current simulator value. The pool radius is kept at least 25 mm smaller than the rim radius: a searched rim or pool radius is drawn only from the part of its range that keeps this gap, so no design needs correcting and a setting that is not ticked is never altered.
  • Flush to optimise: the full or the partial flush. Its volume is the flush volume variable, and water per flush includes any bowl refill.
  • Goal "best trade-off": maximise a weighted score and minimise water. The score is the weighted sum of cleaned area, solids removed and paper removed, with weights set by the user (default 50, 30 and 20 %).
  • Goal "meet targets": the user sets a maximum water use and minimum cleaned, solids and paper shares, and optionally a noise limit in dB against the current design, for example 0 for "no louder than now". The limit must hold at the upper end of the sampled noise range. Among the designs that meet all of them, the tool reports the one with the highest score, the lowest water, or the lowest upper end of the noise range. If none qualifies, it reports the closest design and which targets it misses. Results and the table show each design's noise against the current design, with its range.
  • Constraints: handled by constrained domination. A design that meets all constraints always ranks above one that does not; among designs that miss, the smaller miss ranks higher. A restored water seal is always a constraint.
  • Search: 36 designs per generation over 15 generations (about 540 flushes, about 4 to 7 s), or 60 over 25 generations (about 1,500 flushes) in the thorough setting. Designs that come out identical after snapping to the slider steps are simulated and listed once. It starts from a Latin hypercube. Parents are picked by tournament on rank and crowding distance; children come from blend crossover and occasional Gaussian mutation.
  • Re-rating: changing weights or targets after a run re-ranks the designs already found without running them again.
  • Robust search (optional): rates each design not at its nominal value but at the low end of its spread under the tolerances of the tolerance study. Every design is simulated nominally and as six varied copies, drawn in three mirrored pairs (each deviation once with a plus and once with a minus sign) and with the same deviations for every design, so designs are compared under the same conditions. Cleaning, solids and paper are rated at the mean minus 1.28 standard deviations of the copies, water and noise at the mean plus 1.28 standard deviations, which estimates the values that one copy in ten falls below or above for a normal distribution. A design only counts as restoring the seal, staying in the bowl and finishing if all its copies do. The full-resolution check uses the same six copies. Results say they are rated at the low end of the spread and also give the nominal values. It takes about seven times as long as a normal search (about 80 s for the quick setting). Six copies give only a rough estimate of the spread, but because all designs see the same deviations, the ranking between designs is fairer than the absolute values. Changing a tolerance after a robust search marks it out of date.

The search uses a coarser film update than the main simulator for speed. While it runs, the chart and the table show its quick estimates, and the table says so; the summary with the recommendation appears only at the end. When the search is finished, every design that can be recommended is re-run at the simulator's own resolution and re-rated: the trade-off curve, the best design or the closest one, and the rows of the table. Because re-checking can change the ranking, this repeats until every such design is checked, rating every design against the targets and weights as they are at that moment, so edits made during the check count. The finished recommendations are then taken only from checked designs. The check stops after 200 designs; if that happens, the status says so and better unchecked designs may exist. Two details keep the comparison fair: during the search, a design's quick estimate is compared with the current design's quick estimate, not with its full-resolution result (this matters for a noise limit set against the current design); and designs that miss the targets by little, within about the quick estimate's error of three points in one result, are checked at full resolution too, because they may meet the targets there.

While the search runs, a progress bar shows which step it is in (searching, or checking at full resolution), the generation and the number of designs so far, and the time left. During the search the time left is extrapolated from the time so far. How many designs the check needs is not known in advance, since checking can move new designs into the ranking, so its bar runs against the limit of 200 and the time left is given as a range: from the designs waiting now to the limit. Cancel search stops the search and reports nothing. Stop check ends the check early and reports only the designs checked so far, and the status says so. The tolerance study and the Demo me card show the same kind of bar.

Changing targets or weights re-ranks the designs without a new search and checks again. Until that check is done, the previous results stay visible but greyed out with a note, and the trade-off curve and the best-design star are hidden. Starting a new search cancels any search or check still running and removes the previous summary. Every design remembers the search it came from: a result from an earlier search, or a result whose fixed settings have since changed, is not loaded, and a message says why. Other points in the chart keep their quick estimates, which typically differ by about one point of cleaning but can differ much more in very short flushes; hovering such a point says it is a quick estimate. The optimiser finds the best designs of the model. Its suggestions are only as good as the model's calibration, so they are candidates for a CFD or rig check, not final answers.

Neural surrogate

A fully connected network with 6 inputs (the same design variables, scaled to 0 to 1), two hidden layers of 32 tanh units and 3 linear outputs (cleaned, solids removed and paper removed, scaled to 0 to 1).

  • Data: 100, 300 or 800 training flushes from a Latin hypercube, plus 150 separate test flushes, all computed with the physics model.
  • Training: mean squared error, full batch, Adam with a learning rate of 0.01 that decays tenfold over 1,500 epochs.
  • Checks: mean absolute error and R² on the unseen test designs, and the measured time per prediction against the physics model.

The surrogate and the hybrid calibration below do not cover the noise indicator yet.

With 300 training flushes the network typically reaches an R² around 0.9 to 0.96 on unseen designs and answers in about 5 µs, roughly 1,000 to 2,000 times faster than the physics model. The physics model plays the part that high-fidelity runs (section 13) would play in practice. Trained on such runs, the same kind of network could be faster than a new high-fidelity run by a much larger factor. That is a rough expectation, not a benchmark, and each training run would cost minutes to hours.

Hybrid calibration

An end-to-end run of the calibration workflow in section 13, against synthetic reference data.

  • Reference data: a hidden reference model stands in for high-fidelity runs (section 13). It is the physics model with different coefficients, plus two effects the physics model cannot represent (a penalty away from an optimal nozzle size and a dependence on jet position for cleaning, a low-flow penalty for solids), plus measurement noise of 0.7 points.
  • Calibration: Nelder-Mead fits six coefficients (film friction, spreading, critical shear, shear dose, solids transport threshold, maximum solids concentration) to 4, 6 or 10 reference runs. Coefficients are searched in a bounded log space, for up to 60 iterations.
  • Correction: a Gaussian process with a squared-exponential kernel learns what the calibrated model still gets wrong. The length scale is chosen by leave-one-out error, and the correction is dropped when it would not beat no correction.
  • Test: all three versions, uncalibrated, calibrated and hybrid, are scored on 24 hold-out designs the fit never saw.

In typical runs on this synthetic data, calibration cuts the hold-out error by a factor of about two to eight. With real data the gain is unknown until tried. The correction adds a smaller gain and only when there are enough reference runs to see a pattern in what is left. That matches what to expect with real data: get the physics right first, then let a small data model handle the rest.

Noise calibration

Fits the five noise coefficients to recordings of real flushes, following the procedure in section 10. With synthetic recordings it shows the whole workflow; with the visitor's own recordings it is the real calibration.

  • Recordings: for each design, set up in the simulator, audio files of a few flushes or sound level meter readings (A-weighted, fast, maximum), and optionally the time an air core reaches the trap from a video, or none. Audio files and meter readings have different zero points and cannot be mixed in one fit. Audio is decoded and analysed in the browser and not kept; only the peak levels and the designs are remembered, in this browser.
  • Analysis of audio: the signal is A-weighted with the pole frequencies of IEC 61672-1 (20.6, 107.7, 737.9 and 12,194 Hz), each turned into a digital filter by the bilinear transform with prewarping; up to 10 kHz the weighting is within 0.85 dB of the standard curve. The level is the 125 ms exponential average of the squared signal, as the "fast" setting of a sound level meter, and the peak of a flush is its maximum. Levels are relative to the recording's full scale; only differences are used.
  • Model: each design is simulated once. The noise sources only read the flow (film arrival, outflow, pool level, swirl, siphon state), and the flow does not depend on the noise coefficients, so noiseEval in model.js turns the recorded flow series into the noise result for any coefficients in well under a millisecond. The simulator itself uses the same function, so the two cannot drift apart.
  • Fit: Nelder-Mead in a bounded log space over the slider ranges, from three starting points, minimises the squared difference between the model's and the measured peaks after removing a common offset (microphone, room, distance), plus the air core timing: 0.25 s off counts like 1 dB off, a missing or extra air core like 2 dB. A weak pull towards the current values keeps coefficients that the recordings cannot see from wandering to their limits. The coefficients to fit are chosen by the user; the fall into the outlet is left out by default, because it is best taken from a trapway drawing.
  • Test: with four or more designs, each design is left out in turn, the fit is repeated on the others, and the left-out design is predicted. The root mean square of these errors, with the current and the fitted coefficients, is the main result. Use fitted coefficients sets the values in the noise model sliders.
  • Demo: six designs around the siphonic preset, three synthetic recordings each, made from a hidden reference with different coefficients and things the model does not know: a valve click, fill valve noise after the valve closes, a noise floor, flush-to-flush scatter of about 0.5 dB, and a gurgle that is low-pitched (300 Hz), so A-weighting takes about 7 dB off it. The recordings go through the same audio analysis as real files. In the default run the fit lowers the error on left-out designs from about 0.9 dB to about 0.3 dB, finds the hidden vortex core size, and lowers the trap weight to absorb the A-weighting of the gurgle. The siphonic bowl is used because its gurgle separates the trap sources from the splash sources; in a washdown bowl almost all of the noise comes from the outlet and the recordings say little about the other coefficients.

The time range is an extrapolation. For SPH on GPUs, FIFTY2 reports about 6 minutes for a 1.8 million particle single-phase case over 2 s of flow in PreonLab, and about 45 hours for a 2.2 million particle multiphase case over 5 s. Physics-informed neural networks are not a good fit for forward flush simulation. A review of machine learning for fluid equations found that most papers claiming to beat numerical methods compared against weak baselines, and its authors describe PINNs as much slower than standard numerical methods for forward problems and prone to not converging. Data-driven surrogates trained on solver results are the more promising ML route, at the price of generating the training runs.

16 · Assumptions and limits

  • The oval shape has the same axis ratio at every depth. Real bowls change shape with depth, have an off-centre trap inlet and often an asymmetric back wall.
  • The film is quasi-steady, always attached to the wall, and does not splash, break up or interact with the other jet. Overlaps simply take the higher shear. Each film is one band along a centreline; after part of it has entered the pool, the rest is assumed to move on as a narrower band centred on what is left. A film that climbs back to the rim slides along below the rim lip instead of spilling over.
  • Wall shear comes from a friction-factor law, not from a resolved boundary layer. Its absolute level depends on the calibration of cf.
  • Water reaches the pool after its travel time on the wall. The wall water is a single store with the films' mean travel time as time constant: a sharp front that has reached the pool is smeared, and water on the wall does not keep the properties of the moment it left the nozzle.
  • The basin below the waterline has an assumed shape; published data suggest real bowls hold about twice as much water at rest, and siphonic bowls have larger water surfaces than the siphonic preset (section 4).
  • After a siphon breaks, water left in the trap leg is not returned to the bowl, and priming switches on at once, without the inertia of the water in the leg. The air core in the siphonic preset follows from the draw-down before the break rather than being an independent prediction.
  • The pool rotates as one body. The noise indicator adds an assumed vortex core over the trap to judge when air is drawn in, but this does not feed back into the flow. There is no wave motion.
  • Noise is an experimental, relative indicator from four lumped air entrainment sources in the bowl and trap, not a sound level and not yet checked against recordings. Comparisons across trap types are not robust. Cistern, valve, pipe and building noise are not included. See section 10.
  • The trap is a lumped weir, orifice and siphon model. Its geometry beyond diameter, seal depth and siphon drop is not represented.
  • Waste is two lumped classes. Clogging, media shape, paper tearing and carry along the drain line are not modelled.
  • Splashing, jets hitting each other and spill-over before the pool is full are not modelled, so the model cannot say whether a design splashes. It only detects a pool that reaches the rim.
  • The AI lab uses a conventional neural network surrogate, not a physics-informed network (PINN). The calibration demo runs on synthetic data. Nothing here replaces CFD or tests.
  • The presets are generic EU washdown and siphonic bowls and are not modelled on any manufacturer's product.
  • Status chips on the simulator use display bands chosen for the tool. They are not values from a standard.

18 · Glossary

  • Equivalent radius: radius of a circle with the same area as the oval section.
  • Ovality: long axis divided by short axis of the bowl opening.
  • Shear dose: time integral of wall shear above the critical soil shear, in Pa·s.
  • Seal depth: vertical distance from the weir crest down to the top of the trap inlet. It keeps sewer gases out.
  • Washdown: trap type where water spills over the weir and pushes waste out by momentum. Common in Europe.
  • Siphonic: trap type where the trapway fills, a siphon forms and pulls the pool down. Common in North America.
  • Reduced-order model: a model that keeps the main physical mechanisms in a few equations instead of resolving the full flow field.
  • SPH: smoothed particle hydrodynamics, the meshless particle method used by PreonLab, nanoFluidX, DualSPHysics and the SPH solver of Simcenter STAR-CCM+.
  • VOF: volume of fluid, the mesh-based method that marks the share of water in each cell; used by Ansys Fluent and CFX, Simcenter STAR-CCM+, OpenFOAM and FLOW-3D.
  • PINN: physics-informed neural network, a network trained to satisfy the flow equations directly.
  • Air core: a vortex deep enough that air reaches the outlet through its centre.
  • Minnaert frequency: the natural frequency of a gas bubble in a liquid; it falls as the bubble gets bigger.
  • Fast time weighting: the 125 ms smoothing of a sound level meter's "fast" setting.
